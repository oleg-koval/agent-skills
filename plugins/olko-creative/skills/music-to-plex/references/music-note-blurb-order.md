# Music note blurb order

Use this exact order for artist/album notes:

1. Artist note
2. Album note
3. Optional one-line DJ-style summary

Style:
- Write in the lane of WFMU, LDBK / Radio Brussels, and serious classic underground radio: crate-digger intelligence, warm human enthusiasm, sly humour, and a little beautiful weirdness.
- This is an original radio-DJ voice, not an imitation of any one station or presenter. Never name-drop the style in the note itself.
- Make listeners feel an invitation to listen: sensory, curious, vivid, and slightly dangerous when the record demands it. Prefer concrete musical images over generic praise.
- Include juicy, source-backed facts: recording/release context, label and year, collaborators/producers, samples or scene connections when verified, unusual backstory, chart/cultural impact, and one or two specific listening details. Facts should create intrigue, not become a Wikipedia dump.
- Separate fact from interpretation. If a fact is uncertain or unsourced, omit it or say it is a claim; never invent samples, personnel, labels, dates, or anecdotes.
- Artist note: establish the artist's lane, history, sound, collaborators, and why this artist matters before discussing the particular album.
- Album note: give the release year/label, key players and context, then explain what to listen for and why the record still matters.
- End with an optional one-line DJ cue or listening invitation that is tactile and specific, not promotional copy.
- Avoid reusable AI-blurb phrases and generic metaphors such as “summer breeze,” “bright telescope,” “one sneaker on the pavement,” “the room needs colour,” “key document,” or “still sounds fresh.” If a sentence could describe twenty unrelated albums, rewrite it.
- Do not use a fixed artist-note → atmospheric-album-note → generic-DJ-cue template mechanically. Vary the opening: a scene, an odd fact, a collaborator, a production decision, a historical collision, a lyric-level observation, or a listener challenge.
- Every album note should contain at least two genuinely specific hooks: an unusual verified fact, a named musical relationship explained in context, a concrete production/sample detail, a scene or label connection, or a precise moment to listen for. Names alone are not facts.
- **Editorial attitude:** WFMU is not merely poetic wording. Make the note feel like a live, adventurous radio segment: playful, eccentric, conversational, anti-commercial, and happy to take a funny detour. Let the host sound delighted by the discovery. Use odd connections, affectionate mischief, sharp asides, mini cliffhangers, and unexpected turns when the facts support them.
- The prose may be lopsided, funny, slangy, or slightly unruly. It should sound like a person with a crate, a microphone, and genuine excitement, not a critic delivering a verdict or a label polishing a campaign.
- Facts should enter through personality: “Here’s the strange bit…”, “Listen for what happens after…”, “This is where the record gets gloriously sideways…” Use that energy without forcing these exact phrases every time.
- Ban commercial-review language and prestige fog: “sonic journey,” “immersive experience,” “genre-defining,” “landmark,” “timeless masterpiece,” “a testament to,” and similar claims unless directly quoted and necessary.
- Fun does not mean shallow. Keep the juicy verified facts, but let them trigger curiosity, jokes, and listener invitations rather than sit as a dry credits list.
- Before saving, perform a replaceability test: delete the album and artist names. If most of the note still works for another record, it is too generic and must be rewritten.

Recommended note structure in Obsidian:

```md
# Artist - Album

## Artist note
...

## Album note
...
```
